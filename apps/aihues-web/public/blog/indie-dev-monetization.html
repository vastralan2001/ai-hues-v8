Indie Dev Monetization: From Side Project to $10K MRR

I went from $200/month to $10K MRR in 14 months. Here is what actually worked — and the pricing mistakes that cost me thousands.

2026-05-087 min readAIHues Team

January 2025. My side project was making $217 a month. I was thrilled. Then I talked to a founder friend who asked me a question that changed everything: "Why are you charging $9 a month for something that saves your users 10 hours?"

I did not have a good answer. I had picked $9 because it felt safe. Not because it reflected value. That conversation started a 14-month journey that took me from $217 to $10,000 in monthly recurring revenue.

The Pricing Mistake Everyone Makes

Indie developers are terrible at pricing. We undercharge because we are scared. Scared of rejection. Scared of being called greedy. Scared that if we charge more, nobody will buy.

Here is what I learned: price is a signal of value. When I charged $9, I attracted users who valued my tool at $9. They complained about features. They churned quickly. They never told anyone about it.

When I raised prices to $49, something weird happened. The complaints stopped. The users who stayed were serious. They gave thoughtful feedback. They referred friends. Because at $49, my tool needed to deliver $49 of value — and it did.

"Your price filters your customers. Low prices filter for price-sensitive users who will leave for the next $5 deal. Higher prices filter for users who actually need what you built."

The Pricing Framework That Worked

I moved to a three-tier model. Not because some blog told me to. Because my users literally asked for it.

The free tier was limited — 5 uses per month, no support. It existed purely for discovery and SEO. About 80% of signups stayed on free. That was fine.

The Pro tier was $29 a month. Full features, email support, basic analytics. This was for individual power users. It became 60% of my revenue.

The Team tier was $99 a month. Shared workspaces, admin controls, priority support. I thought nobody would buy it. Three teams signed up in the first month. I should have added it sooner.

The Growth Funnel Nobody Talks About

Reaching $10K MRR requires roughly 200 customers at $49, or 100 at $99. Sounds impossible? It is not. It just takes longer than you want.

Months 1-3: validation. I focused on getting 10 paying customers. Any way possible. Twitter DMs. Indie Hackers posts. Emailing friends. I did not care about scale. I cared about proof.

Months 4-8: product-market fit. I talked to every single customer. Why did they sign up? What almost made them leave? What would make them pay double? I recorded these calls and listened to them while walking my dog.

Months 9-14: scale. I doubled down on what worked. For me, that was SEO content. I wrote one deep-dive post per week targeting long-tail keywords. Each post brought in 5-10 signups per month. Compound interest for traffic.

What I Would Do Differently

I chased enterprise too early. A Fortune 500 company reached out in month 6. I got excited. Spent 3 weeks on a custom demo. They ghosted me. Enterprise sales cycles kill indie devs. Do not chase them until you have a team.

I ignored churn for too long. I was so focused on new signups that I did not notice 15% of my users leaving every month. Fixing churn — better onboarding, clearer value proposition — doubled my effective growth rate.

And I waited too long to raise prices. My existing customers did not care when I went from $9 to $29. New customers did not blink at $49. The only people who complained were the ones who never would have paid anyway.

The Real Metric

Here is the thing about $10K MRR. It sounds like a magic number. It is not. It is just the point where I could quit my day job. That is it.

The real metric that mattered? Whether my customers would be disappointed if my tool disappeared tomorrow. When I hit 50% of users saying "very disappointed," I knew I had something real. Everything else — revenue, growth, metrics — followed from that.

Build something people genuinely need. Charge what it is worth. Talk to your customers every week. The rest is just math.
