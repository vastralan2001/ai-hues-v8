The 2026 AI Productivity Stack

I cut my tool stack from 23 apps to 7. Here is what actually moved the needle — and what I deleted.

2026-05-105 min readAIHues Team

Last year, I had 23 productivity apps. Twenty-three. I spent more time managing my tools than doing actual work. Every Monday morning was a ritual of checking dashboards, syncing integrations, and wondering why nothing felt productive.

So I did something drastic. I deleted everything and started over with one rule: one tool per job. No exceptions. No "but this one has a slightly better feature." One tool. Use it properly.

Here is what survived the purge.

Notion: The Central Nervous System

I had tried Obsidian, Craft, Capacities, and about six others. They were all great. But Notion won because my team already used it. The switching cost of moving everyone was higher than any feature gap.

I use Notion for documents, project plans, meeting notes, and my personal wiki. The AI features are hit-or-miss, but they are good enough for summarizing long docs and extracting action items. I do not use it for writing — just organizing.

One trick that saved me hours: I have a template for every recurring meeting. Agenda, notes, action items. No more blank-page syndrome.

Slack + Claude: The Communication Hack

Slack is fine. It is just chat. But here is my secret weapon: I feed Claude my Slack threads from the day and ask it to extract decisions, unresolved questions, and action items.

It sounds ridiculous. It works ridiculously well. I went from spending 45 minutes reading threads to 5 minutes reviewing Claude's summary. The 40 minutes I save? I use that for deep work.

"The best productivity hack is not doing more. It is removing the stuff that feels like work but is not."

Cursor: The Only IDE I Need

I switched from VS Code to Cursor six months ago. I have not looked back. The AI autocomplete is good. The command-K inline editing is great. But the real killer feature? Tab-complete that actually understands my codebase.

I used to context-switch between my editor and ChatGPT constantly. Now I barely leave Cursor. It is not perfect — sometimes it suggests code that looks right but breaks things. But it is right often enough that I am easily 2x faster than before.

Figma: Design Without the Designer

I am not a designer. But Figma's AI features let me fake it convincingly. I can describe a landing page and get a decent first draft. I can remove backgrounds from photos in one click. I can generate icon variations.

Is it as good as hiring a designer? No. Is it good enough for MVP-stage products? Absolutely. I have shipped three landing pages this year without touching a design tool beyond Figma.

Make: The Glue

Every repetitive task in my business is automated. New blog post? Auto-share to Twitter, LinkedIn, and newsletter. New user signs up? Welcome email, Slack notification, Airtable update. All of it runs without me.

I have 14 active automations. They run about 200 times per week. Total time I spend on them per month? Maybe 30 minutes of maintenance.

The rule is simple: if I do it more than twice, I automate it. No exceptions.

What I Deleted

Second brain apps. I had three. Now I have one page in Notion. Simpler is better.

Time trackers. I tracked every minute for a month. Learned nothing useful. Deleted them all.

Multiple note apps. Apple Notes, Bear, Drafts. I was spreading my thoughts across five apps and could never find anything. One app. One inbox. Done.

The Philosophy

Here is what I learned from cutting 23 apps to 7: productivity is not about having the best tools. It is about having few enough tools that you actually know how to use them.

Every app you add creates friction. Context switching. Forgotten passwords. Sync conflicts. The goal is not optimization. It is elimination.

Pick your stack. Learn it deeply. Ignore everything else.
