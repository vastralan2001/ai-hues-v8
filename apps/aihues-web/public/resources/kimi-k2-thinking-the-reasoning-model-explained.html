Inside Kimi K2 Thinking: How an Open Model Learns to Reason

One trillion parameters, a 256K window, and a habit of thinking for hundreds of steps before it answers. Here is how it actually works, told the way it reasons.

2026-06-259 min readAIHues Team

Source: Read the model card ↗

Give a language model a question it cannot answer in one breath. Something like: find the three most-cited papers that disagree with a 2019 result, then check whether any of them were later retracted. No single fact answers that. The model has to search, read, hold a partial answer in its head, search again, and notice when a new page contradicts what it believed four steps ago. Most models fall apart somewhere in the middle. They drift, repeat a tool call they already made, or quietly forget the original question.

Kimi K2 Thinking, released by Moonshot AI in November 2025, was built specifically for the part where other models drift. It is a reasoning model: it produces an explicit chain of thought, calls tools partway through that thinking, reads the results, and keeps going. What makes it notable is not that it thinks out loud. Plenty of models do. It is how far it can think without losing the thread, and that it does all of this with open weights anyone can download.

Watch it work through one hard problem

Picture the retraction question above. K2 Thinking does not jump to an answer. It first writes out a plan in its reasoning trace: identify the 2019 result, find papers that cite it negatively, then cross-check retraction notices. Then it issues a search. The result comes back, it reads it, and crucially it folds that reading back into the same continuous train of thought rather than starting fresh. A contradiction on page four does not reset the whole effort. It updates the working answer and presses on.

The mechanism that allows this is what Moonshot calls interleaved thinking and tool use. The model alternates between private reasoning steps and external actions, dozens or hundreds of times, inside a single response. Moonshot reports that K2 Thinking holds coherent, goal-directed behavior across 200 to 300 sequential tool calls. That figure is the headline, because it is exactly where earlier agents broke. Models that looked impressive in a demo would degrade after 30 to 50 steps, looping or wandering off task. K2 Thinking was trained to stay on the rope much longer.

The hard part of reasoning is not the first step. It is the three-hundredth step still pointing at the question you started with.

The architecture underneath

K2 Thinking is a sparse Mixture-of-Experts model. On paper it is huge. In practice each token only touches a small slice of it. The numbers that matter:

Property Value
Total parameters ~1 trillion (MoE)
Activated per token ~32 billion
Experts 384 total, 8 routed + 1 shared
Context window 256K tokens
Native precision INT4 (quantization-aware training)
Checkpoint size at INT4 ~594 GB

The trick of MoE is the gap between those first two rows. K2 Thinking has the knowledge capacity of a trillion-parameter model, but a router picks only 8 of 384 experts for each token, so the cost of generating a token is closer to a 32B dense model. You get breadth without paying for it on every forward pass. The 256K window is what lets all those tool results, search snippets, and intermediate notes sit in context at once, so step 290 can still see what step 12 found.

Why the model ships in 4-bit on purpose

Most quantized models are squeezed after training. You train in high precision, then round the weights down to fit on cheaper hardware, and you lose a little quality in the rounding. K2 Thinking inverts that. Moonshot used quantization-aware training, so the model learned to operate in INT4 from the start. The 4-bit representation is not a compromise bolted on at the end; it is the format the weights were optimized for.

This buys two concrete things. Memory roughly halves against an FP8 version, which is how a trillion-parameter model fits in about 594 GB instead of a full terabyte. And generation runs around twice as fast, which matters enormously when a single answer might involve hundreds of reasoning-plus-tool turns. The choice of INT4 over the newer FP4 was deliberate and unglamorous: pre-Blackwell NVIDIA GPUs, including the widely deployed A100 and H100, lack native FP4 hardware. INT4 runs on the cards people already own.

One detail worth keeping honest: the benchmark scores below were all measured at INT4. This is not a high-precision model showing off and a degraded version shipping to users. The reported numbers and the downloadable weights are the same thing.

Test-time scaling: thinking longer, and thinking eight times

The most interesting lever K2 Thinking pulls is at inference, not training. Reasoning models improve when you let them spend more compute per question, and Moonshot exposes this in two ways.

The first is simply the depth of the reasoning-and-tool loop. Letting the model run more interleaved steps on a genuinely hard task tends to raise accuracy, up to the point where the context window fills. The second is a mode Moonshot calls Heavy. For the hardest benchmark runs it rolls out eight reasoning trajectories in parallel, then reflectively aggregates them into one final answer. Eight independent attempts catch mistakes a single pass would commit to. It is expensive, and it is reserved for cases where being right matters more than being cheap, but it shows the ceiling moves when you spend more at test time rather than retraining.

The benchmark numbers, in context

K2 Thinking's claim to attention is that it is the first open-weights model to post state-of-the-art results against closed frontier models on several reasoning and agentic benchmarks. The headline figures Moonshot reports:

  • Humanity's Last Exam (HLE): 44.9%. A deliberately brutal academic exam where most models score in the teens or low twenties.
  • BrowseComp: 60.2%. A web-browsing agent benchmark that rewards exactly the long, self-correcting search behavior described above.
  • SWE-Bench Verified: 71.3%. Resolving real GitHub issues by editing real codebases, a test of sustained multi-file reasoning.

The pattern across those three is consistent and tells you what the model is for. They are not single-shot trivia. Each one rewards an agent that can plan, act, observe, and revise over a long horizon. The BrowseComp result in particular is the benchmark expression of the 200-to-300-call durability, and it is where an open model leading the field is genuinely surprising.

The honest limits

None of this makes K2 Thinking a free lunch. The most immediate barrier is that you cannot casually run it. Even at INT4 the checkpoint is roughly 594 GB, and serving it comfortably wants well over 512 GB of system memory plus capable GPUs. This is a model you reach through an API or a serious cluster, not your laptop. Day-zero support exists for inference engines such as vLLM, SGLang, and KTransformers, but the hardware floor is real.

Latency is the second cost. A model that genuinely reasons across hundreds of steps is, by construction, slower than one that blurts an answer. The INT4 speedup helps, and Heavy mode's eight parallel trajectories multiply the spend further. For a quick factual lookup, all that deliberation is overkill. The long-horizon machinery earns its keep on hard, multi-step problems and wastes time on easy ones.

And the headline reliability figure is a ceiling, not a floor. Holding the thread for 200 to 300 tool calls is a reported capability under good conditions, not a guarantee for every chain. Agents still accumulate small errors over long runs, and a benchmark score is not a promise about your specific workflow. Treat the durability as a strong tendency that needs verification on your own tasks, not a contract.

Why it matters for builders

For anyone building agents, Kimi K2 Thinking changes the supply picture more than the capability picture. The capability, deep reasoning interleaved with tool use, already existed behind closed APIs. What is new is that comparable behavior now ships with open weights you can inspect, fine-tune, and self-host, with the long-horizon tool-calling and 256K context that real agentic work actually needs. If your product depends on an agent that can plan across many steps without losing the plot, the practical question shifts from "can a model do this" to "do I rent it or run it." That is a healthier question to be asking, and K2 Thinking is a large part of why it is now on the table.
