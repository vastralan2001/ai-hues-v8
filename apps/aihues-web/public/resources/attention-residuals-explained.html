Attention Residuals, Explained — and the Teenager Who Co-Wrote It

Moonshot's new architecture lets each transformer layer choose which earlier layers to listen to. One of its two lead authors is a high-schooler from Shenzhen.

When Moonshot AI posted a short paper called Attention Residuals in March 2026, two things happened quickly. Elon Musk and Andrej Karpathy both stopped to look. And word got out that one of its two co-first authors, Nathan Chen, was still in high school. The idea is small enough to explain in a paragraph and consequential enough that a frontier lab put it into a real training run. This is what it says, why it works, and how a teenager ended up with his name on it.

The problem: a residual stream that never stops growing

Modern transformers are built on residual connections. Each layer reads the running sum of everything before it, computes something new, and adds its result back onto the pile. With the now-standard PreNorm setup, that pile is accumulated with fixed, uniform weights — every layer's output is added in at full strength, forever.

That sounds harmless. It is not, at depth. The hidden state grows larger and larger as you stack layers, and the relative contribution of any single layer shrinks into the noise. A 60th layer trying to correct a subtle mistake made at layer 12 has to shout over the accumulated sum of everything in between. The "residual highway" that makes deep networks trainable also dilutes them.

The idea: let each layer attend over depth

Attention Residuals (AttnRes) changes one thing. Instead of adding every previous layer's output with the same fixed weight, each layer runs a small softmax attention over the outputs of the layers before it — and decides, per input, how much of each earlier representation to pull in.

The authors frame it as a duality between time and depth. Attention across a sequence lets a token decide which other tokens matter; attention across depth lets a layer decide which earlier layers matter. The residual connection stops being a dumb running total and becomes a learned, input-dependent mixture. A layer can now reach back past the noise and weight the one representation it actually needs.

Making it scale: Block AttnRes

Attending over every preceding layer is expensive — it adds memory and cross-device communication that a trillion-token training run cannot afford. So the paper introduces Block AttnRes: split the layers into blocks and attend only over block-level representations. It keeps most of the benefit of full AttnRes while cutting the overhead back down to something a real pre-training pipeline will tolerate.

This is the part that separates a clever idea from a usable one. Plenty of architecture tweaks look good on a toy model and quietly fall apart when you try to train at scale. Block AttnRes exists precisely so the idea survives contact with a production run.

Does it actually work?

Moonshot tested it on Kimi Linear — a 48B-total, 3B-activated model trained on 1.4 trillion tokens. The headline result: Block AttnRes delivered roughly a 1.25× compute advantage, matching a baseline that was trained with about 25% more resources. On downstream evaluations against that baseline it improved:

  • GPQA-Diamond by +7.5 points
  • Math by +3.6 points
  • HumanEval by +3.1 points

A 25% effective compute saving on a frontier-scale run is not a rounding error — it is the kind of number that pays for a research team. That is why a one-idea paper got the attention it did.

The teenager behind it

Nathan Chen — Guangyu Chen — is a co-first author on the paper and, at the time of writing, a high-school student from Shenzhen. He did not arrive at Moonshot through a PhD program. He got there by doing the work in the open: contributing to the Flash Linear Attention project, the open-source library that a lot of modern efficient-attention work builds on. Good commits in a visible repository are their own résumé, and the Kimi team noticed.

The rest of the path fits the same shape. He had been a Codeforces Candidate Master with a 2000-plus rating — competitive programming, the long way round — and had spent time at Tilde Research before joining Moonshot. None of it is the conventional credential. All of it is the kind of thing you can only get by building, publicly, before anyone is paying you to.

The interesting lesson is not "a teenager did this." It is that the shortest route into a frontier lab right now runs through open-source contribution, not a transcript.

Why it matters

Attention Residuals is a reminder that there is still low-hanging fruit in the most-studied part of the stack. The residual connection has been treated as settled since 2015. Asking a basic question about it — what if the weights weren't fixed? — turned out to be worth a quarter of a training budget.

For builders, there is nothing to integrate today; this is an architecture change that lives inside the model, not an API. But it is worth tracking, because gains like this tend to show up in the next generation of open weights — and because it is a clean example of where the field's real edges still are.
